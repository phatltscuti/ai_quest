A hands-on Gemini Notebook test with four fictional PDF manuals for the AeroPure AQ-200 air purifier: two generated sites (troubleshooting guide and dashboard), ten scored questions, and a criterion-by-criterion comparison against a Python RAG pipeline built on the same documents.

About the data: every document used in this post (the AeroPure AQ-200 and "Nimbus Home Appliances") is fictional and was generated by my own script. Every PDF page carries the banner "FICTIONAL SAMPLE – FOR DEMO ONLY". No internal or customer information was used in the demo or during testing.

How this post answers the four requirements

The quest asks for four things. To make it easy to check that each one is covered, here is where each requirement is answered:

1. What "make me a site" does, and why I tested it on a product manual

In Gemini Notebook (notebook.google.com) you add sources and type make me a site ... into the chat. Instead of a written answer, Notebook generates a complete HTML/JavaScript web app, saves it as an item in the Studio panel, and lets you open it in its own tab or share it.

Other posts have already used this feature to build marketing dashboards. This post takes a different angle, closer to the day-to-day reality of a software company: turning product manuals and technical documents into interactive tools (error-code lookup, maintenance checklist, cost calculator), and then measuring objectively how that differs from what development teams usually build for the same problem: a traditional RAG pipeline (chunk → embed → retrieve → LLM answer).

  • Notebook path: 4 PDF manuals → Gemini Notebook → "Make me a site" → interactive web app
  • RAG path: 4 PDF manuals → pypdf + chunking → gemini-embedding-2 → top-5 cosine retrieval → gemini-3.5-flash-lite answer with [S#] citations

2. Source material: a fictional four-PDF manual set

I wrote a small Python script that holds all product data in one place as the single source of truth and renders both Markdown and PDF versions from it. Because of that, every correct answer (ground truth) can be recomputed in code instead of being judged by eye.

Why the manuals were designed this way

The four documents are small on purpose, but each one is built to stress a known weak point of document-based AI systems, so that the comparison later has something to measure:

  • Facts that live in tables. Specifications, error codes, maintenance intervals, prices and monthly figures are all tables, because tables are where PDF text extraction is weakest and where an interactive UI (lookup, comparison, calculator, chart) is most useful.
  • Questions that need two documents. The error-code table names a part (FL-200H), but its price is only in the price list. The sales report gives units sold, but the unit list prices ($149 / $229 / $349) are only in the user manual's spec table. Both force a join across files.
  • Model-specific rules. Most codes apply to all models, E11 and E12 (Wi-Fi, firmware) only to the AQ-200 and Pro, and two codes only to the Pro (E08 VOC sensor, E13 UV-C lamp). That tests filtering, not just lookup.
  • Numbers that must be added up. Ten months × three models of sales and ten months × five ticket categories allow aggregation questions whose answers I can recompute exactly.
  • Notes that explain anomalies. The report notes explain the March Wi-Fi spike (firmware 2.3, fixed by 2.3.1 on 28 March) and the August sensor spike (high humidity, more E06 "sensor dirty" tickets), so a good dashboard should connect numbers to causes.
  • A product that does not exist. Nothing about an "AQ-300" appears anywhere, which gives a clean hallucination test.

Each document also carries a document number (AQ200-UM-01, AQ200-TS-02, AQ200-PL-03, AQ200-RP-04) and "Revision 1.0 (Nov 2026)", like real controlled documentation, so I could see whether the generated pages refer to their sources by document. (The dashboard did: its methodology footer names AQ200-RP-04, AQ200-PL-03 and AQ200-UM-01.) The generator renders the same content twice: as Markdown, which is easy to read and review, and as A4 PDF, which is what I actually uploaded because it is closer to what a customer would send. In total the set is small: the RAG extractor in section 6 turned it into 17 chunks and 4,430 embedding tokens.

3. Data safety: how I kept confidential information out

Requirement 2 of this quest is strict, so I treated it as a design constraint from the start rather than a check at the end. The short version: nothing in this experiment came from a real company, product, customer or colleague, and I checked that at every stage where data could leak.

Where the data came from

All product data is typed by hand into one Python file, product_data.py, whose header states: "Everything here is invented for a demo. No real company, product, customer or internal data." The company name is literally "Nimbus Home Appliances (fictional)", with the word fictional inside the name, and the AeroPure AQ-200 product line is invented. Prices, sales figures, ticket counts and CSAT scores are made-up numbers chosen to be internally consistent; they are not anonymised versions of real numbers. That distinction matters: anonymised real data can still reveal business information through trends, ratios or seasonality, while invented data cannot.

What I checked, stage by stage

"Non-sensitive" also applies to the output. The generated sites run on Google's infrastructure (a sandboxed frame on a *.usercontent.goog domain) and live under my Google account, so I treat them like any other cloud document: fine for fictional or public material, not acceptable for anything under NDA. That rule carries over into how I plan to use the feature at work (section 11).

4. Step by step: generating the site in Gemini Notebook

Everything below was done in a real Chrome session (Gemini Notebook PRO account) on 20 November 2026. Button names are quoted exactly as they appear in the UI.

Step 1: Create a notebook

Go to https://notebook.google.com/ and click + New notebook. (My personal notebook list is blurred.)

Step 2: Rename it and open Add sources

The new notebook is called "Untitled notebook". Click the title and rename it to AQ-200 Demo – Interactive Site. Click + Add sources; the dialog offers Upload files, Websites, Play Books, Drive, Textbooks, Copied text.

Step 3: Upload the PDFs

Choose Upload files and select the four PDFs. Measured: about 8.5 seconds until all four sources were listed in the Sources panel. Notebook writes a source summary right after.

Step 4: Prompt "Make me a site"

Type the prompt into the "Ask a question or create something" box:

Make me a site: an interactive troubleshooting and user-guide website for the AeroPure AQ-200 air purifier series, based only on these sources. Include: (1) an error-code lookup where I can type or pick a code E01-E15 and see the meaning, cause, fix, which models it applies to, and the spare part and price if a part is needed; (2) a side-by-side comparison of the 3 models; (3) a maintenance checklist with checkboxes grouped by interval, plus a yearly filter cost calculator (model + normal/pets usage); (4) a searchable FAQ. Clean, modern, mobile-friendly design.

Step 5: Wait for the Studio item

The chat replies almost immediately: "I've started generating your interactive troubleshooting and user-guide site … in your Studio panel". On the right, Studio shows a "Generating Site… / based on 4 sources" card. Measured: still running at 182 s and finished at 212 s, so roughly 3 to 3.5 minutes.

Step 6: Open the site

Click the card in Studio. The site opens in a viewer with Open in new tab, Collapse, More options (⋮) and Close buttons, plus View 4 sources and Good content / Bad content.

Step 7: Open in new tab

Click Open in new tab. The site gets its own URL (…/notebook/<id>/artifact/<id>) with To notebook and Create your own in the top bar. The content runs in a sandboxed iframe on a *.usercontent.goog domain, and the footer reads "Generated by AI based on user-provided sources and instructions".

What I tested on site A

Inside site A: what Notebook actually generated

Gemini titled the page "AeroPure AQ-200 Series — Troubleshooting & User Guide" and organised it into four tabs that mirror the four parts of my prompt: Error-code lookup, Compare models, Maintenance & filter cost and Searchable FAQ. Beyond what I asked for, it added:

  • A "Filter by model" switch (All / Lite / AQ-200 / Pro) on the error-code grid, so a Lite owner does not have to read Pro-only codes.
  • A "No error code on display? Check common symptoms" block that turns the four symptom rows of the troubleshooting PDF into clickable entries.
  • A "Required spare parts & service pricing (2026 USD)" panel on every code card, and a safety reminder ("Always unplug the purifier before opening any cover") on codes such as E01.
  • A control-panel quick reference and a "Placement & safety notes" box (no bathrooms, 30 cm clearance, 1 m from heaters, 10-minute sensor calibration) that I never requested.
  • Summary cards per model (price, CADR, room size, warranty) above the full spec table, with the RC-200 remote's $15 price joined into the comparison row from the price list.
  • In the calculator, the number of filters per year, the replacement interval and a monthly average (for example $5.83 per month for the Lite in normal use); a checklist that can be filtered by model; FAQ categories (Operation, Features, Care & Filters, Warranty & Service) and an "Expand all" button.

I saved the generated page and read its source. It is a single, self-contained HTML file (about 165 KB) styled with Tailwind. All content is embedded as JavaScript constants — ERROR_CODES, SYMPTOMS, COMPARISON_ROWS, MAINTENANCE_TASKS and FAQS — and the UI is rendered from them. The page sets a strict Content-Security-Policy (default-src 'none' plus a short allow-list of CDNs), and I found no call to a language model or retrieval service in it. This is the key technical fact behind the comparison later: when someone uses the site, no AI runs; the knowledge has been compiled into data at generation time. One entry looks like this:

{
  code: `E02`,
  meaning: `Filter life expired`,
  cause: `Main filter has reached its rated service life (about 2,160 operating hours).`,
  steps: [
    `Unplug the purifier and open the filter compartment.`,
    `Install a new main filter (FL-200H for Lite/AQ-200, FL-200P for Pro) with all plastic packaging removed.`,
    `Close the cover, plug the unit in, and press and hold Filter Reset for 3 seconds to reset the filter life counter.`
  ],
  models: `All models`,
  modelKeys: [`lite`, `std`, `pro`],
  parts: [`FL-200H`, `FL-200H-2`, `FL-200P`],
  serviceNote: `Consumable item: main filters are not covered by the warranty.`
}

Note the parts field: the error-code PDF only names FL-200H and FL-200P, and Gemini added the FL-200H-2 value pack from the price list on its own, along with the warranty note from the price list footer. That is exactly the cross-document join a RAG system has to perform at every query — here it was done once, at generation time, and can be reviewed like any other data.

5. Second site: a sales and support dashboard

Make me a site: an interactive sales & support dashboard for the AeroPure AQ-200 series using only the monthly report (Jan-Oct 2026) and the price list. Include KPI cards (total units, revenue at list price, total tickets, average CSAT), a monthly units chart by model with a model filter, a stacked support-ticket chart by category, a CSAT vs resolution-time trend, a month range selector, and callouts that explain the March Wi-Fi spike and the August sensor spike from the report notes.

The chat confirmed after 16.8 s and the site was ready within 157 s (about 2.5 minutes) of sending the prompt. While it was generating I kept asking questions in the chat; generation runs in the background and does not block the chat.

An interesting finding: when I asked the chat for "total units in Q1", Notebook answered 3,490, which is wrong, even though the three monthly totals it listed were all correct. The dashboard that the same Notebook generated shows 3,790, which is right, because that number is computed by JavaScript from the data table embedded in the page rather than added up in the LLM's head. That is the most important difference I found: for numbers, the site is more reliable than the chat answer.

What else the dashboard showed, and where it took liberties

Gemini titled it "AeroPure AQ-200 Series — Sales & Support Dashboard (Jan–Oct 2026)" and split it into three sections — unit volume and list-price revenue, support volume and service quality, and a source-tables ledger — plus the two callout panels I asked for. I recomputed the following against the report, and all of it matches:

  • Peak month July 2026 with 2,320 units; model mix AQ-200 48.2% (8,275 units, $1,894,975), Lite 32.7% (5,615 units, $836,635), Pro 19.1% (3,285 units, $1,146,465).
  • A "blended ASP" of $225.80 per unit (revenue ÷ units) — a metric I did not request, but correctly computed.
  • Ticket mix: Filter 505 (43.0%), Wi-Fi/App 280, Sensor 174, Noise 123, Other 93.
  • All ten rows of the monthly ledger (units, list revenue, tickets, resolution time, CSAT), from Jan (1,240 units, $276,360, 91 tickets) to Oct (1,680 units, $384,320, 111 tickets).
  • Range presets All, Q1 (Jan–Mar), Peak (Jun–Aug) and Jul–Oct; a Stacked/Grouped toggle on the units chart; a ticket-category filter; sortable ledger columns; and an honest note that tickets, resolution time and CSAT are reported for the whole series, so the model filter does not apply to them.

Three behaviours are worth knowing before you show a generated dashboard to a client:

  • It quietly used a third document. My prompt said "using only the monthly report and the price list", but unit list prices are not in the price list (it only covers spare parts and service); they are in the user manual's spec table. The dashboard took them from there and said so in its "Source Attribution & Methodology" footer. The result is correct and transparent, but it did not follow the letter of the prompt.
  • It chose its own formulas. Average CSAT (4.27) and average resolution time (23.4 h) are weighted by monthly ticket volume; the simple means are 4.29 and 23.1 h. The footer states this, and the code confirms it (a weighted CSAT sum divided by total tickets). Neither choice is wrong, but it is a choice someone should make consciously.
  • It added interpretation. The ledger labels months with notes such as "Baseline", "Pre-peak ramp", "Annual unit peak" and "Stabilized", and a caption says that "resolution delays directly depress satisfaction". The fact that the CSAT troughs (March, August) coincide with the slowest resolution times is in the data; the causal wording is Gemini's, not the report's.

Technically the dashboard is the same kind of artifact as site A: one HTML file (about 90 KB) with the whole report embedded as a MONTHLY_DATA array and the charts drawn with Chart.js 4.5.1. Every KPI is recalculated in the browser when you change the range, which is why it can get Q1 right when the chat could not.

6. Building a traditional RAG baseline on the same documents

For a fair comparison I wrote a minimal RAG pipeline in Python on exactly the same four PDFs:

  • Extract: pypdf pulls the text of each page.
  • Chunk: lines are grouped into ~900-character chunks with a 2-line overlap, giving 17 chunks.
  • Embed: gemini-embedding-2 (3,072 dimensions, 4,430 tokens). A practical gotcha: this model is multimodal and merges a list of contents into a single vector, so each chunk has to be embedded in its own call. My first batched attempt returned one vector for all 17 chunks.
  • Retrieve: cosine similarity, top 5.
  • Answer: gemini-3.5-flash-lite, temperature 0, required to cite [S#] and to reply "Not found in the documents." when the context does not contain the answer.

The core of the pipeline, so you can reproduce it on your own documents:

# Minimal RAG baseline (Python, google-genai, pypdf, numpy)
from google import genai
from google.genai import types
from pypdf import PdfReader
import numpy as np

c = genai.Client(api_key=GEMINI_API_KEY)

# 1) Extract + chunk (~900 chars, 2-line overlap)
chunks = []
for pdf in PDF_FILES:
    for pno, page in enumerate(PdfReader(pdf).pages, 1):
        buf = []
        for line in [l.strip() for l in page.extract_text().splitlines() if l.strip()]:
            if buf and sum(map(len, buf)) + len(line) > 900:
                chunks.append({"src": f"{pdf} p.{pno}", "text": "\n".join(buf)}); buf = buf[-2:]
            buf.append(line)
        if buf: chunks.append({"src": f"{pdf} p.{pno}", "text": "\n".join(buf)})

# 2) Embed - one call per chunk (gemini-embedding-2 merges a list into ONE vector)
def embed(text, task):
    r = c.models.embed_content(model="gemini-embedding-2", contents=text,
                               config=types.EmbedContentConfig(task_type=task))
    return np.array(r.embeddings[0].values)
M = np.array([embed(ch["text"], "RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT") for ch in chunks])
M /= np.linalg.norm(M, axis=1, keepdims=True)

# 3) Retrieve top-5 by cosine + answer with citations
SYSTEM = ("Answer using ONLY the numbered context chunks. Cite them like [S1]. "
          "If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: Not found in the documents.")
def ask(question, k=5):
    q = embed(question, "RETRIEVAL_QUERY"); q /= np.linalg.norm(q)
    top = np.argsort(-(M @ q))[:k]
    ctx = "\n\n".join(f"[S{r+1}] ({chunks[i]['src']})\n{chunks[i]['text']}" for r, i in enumerate(top))
    return c.models.generate_content(
        model="gemini-3.5-flash-lite", contents=f"Context:\n{ctx}\n\nQuestion: {question}",
        config=types.GenerateContentConfig(system_instruction=SYSTEM, temperature=0)).text

print(ask("Which month had the most Wi-Fi/App support tickets, how many, and what was the reason?"))

Measured: index build in 10.1 s (extract + chunk 0.04 s, embedding 10 s). Each question took 5.1–7.0 s. Total cost for 10 questions: $0.0062 (16,553 input / 497 output tokens at the flash-lite rate of $0.30 / $2.50 per 1M tokens; the embedding cost for 4,430 tokens is not included).

How the baseline works, piece by piece

The snippet above is a condensed version. The full script, rag.py (about 200 lines), has three commands:

python rag.py build                          # extract -> chunk -> embed -> save index.json
python rag.py ask "question" -k 5             # retrieve top-k + answer with citations
python rag.py eval ../eval/questions.json    # run all 10 test questions, save every answer
  • Extraction: whitespace is normalised, and the "FICTIONAL SAMPLE" banner line is dropped before chunking so that the same sentence, repeated on every page, does not end up in every vector.
  • Chunking: lines are appended until the next one would push the chunk past 900 characters; the last two lines are carried into the next chunk to keep a little context (for example a table header). Chunks never cross page boundaries. The result is 17 chunks of 179–927 characters: 2 from the price list, 2 from the sales report, 5 from the troubleshooting guide and 8 from the user manual.
  • Embedding: the RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT task type for chunks and RETRIEVAL_QUERY for questions; vectors are L2-normalised so that a dot product equals cosine similarity.
  • Prompt: the system instruction (below) forbids outside knowledge, requires a citation after each fact, demands an exact refusal string and asks for the numbers behind any calculation.
  • Logging: every answer is saved together with the IDs and similarity scores of the retrieved chunks, the retrieval and generation times, token counts and cost. That log is what makes the failure analysis in section 7 possible.
You answer questions about a product using ONLY the numbered context chunks.
Rules:
- Cite the chunks you used like [S1], [S3] right after each fact.
- If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: "Not found in the documents." and nothing else.
- For calculations, show the numbers you used. Be concise (max 6 sentences).

Where the time goes: retrieval (embedding the question plus one matrix product) took 0.39–0.57 s per question, and generation took 4.6–6.4 s, so almost all of the latency is the LLM call. Each question sent between 1,314 and 2,043 input tokens. Index building is dominated by the 17 sequential embedding calls; the run reported above took 10.1 s, and a later rebuild of the same index logged 12.1 s.

7. Results: 10 questions

The ten questions cover simple lookups, multi-step questions that join two documents, aggregations over the sales table, and one question that is not answerable from the documents (the AQ-300) to test hallucination. The same English questions went to the RAG script and to the Notebook chat (the same notebook that generated the sites). I scored them by hand against the source data.

How to read these results: my RAG is a minimal build (character-based chunking, top-5, the cheapest model, a very strict prompt). It is "safe" (it invented nothing) but gives up too often. Matching Notebook would take more work: structure-aware table parsing, chunking per row or per error code, hybrid search (BM25 + vectors), reranking, a larger k… That is the hidden cost of a home-grown RAG.

Question by question: what each side actually said

The scores hide the most interesting part, so here is what each system actually answered, together with the retrieval log of the RAG run.

Q1 – E03. RAG: "Not found in the documents." The top-ranked chunk (similarity 0.854) was the first chunk of the troubleshooting table, and it ends exactly after E03's meaning and cause ("Plastic bag not removed from a new filter; shows within the first 30 minutes of use"). The fix line sits in the next chunk, which did not make the top 5. With half an answer in hand, the strict prompt made the model refuse. Notebook: meaning, cause, "All models", four fix steps (unplug, open the cover, remove the bag, reinstall and restart) and "Parts Needed: None", with a reference number on each sentence.

Q2 – Pro CADR and room size. Both correct. The RAG answer wrote the units as LaTeX ("450 $m^3/h$", "38-55 $m^2$") — a small formatting artifact you would have to render or strip in a real UI. Notebook gave the same numbers and offered to build a comparison chart of the three models.

Q3 – E02 on the Lite. Both correct. RAG: FL-200H, $39, hold Filter Reset for 3 seconds, citing [S1, S3] (the error-code chunk and the price-list chunk). Notebook added the $70 FL-200H-2 two-pack and a reminder to remove the packaging from the new filter.

Q4 – Pro with two cats. RAG refused. The retrieved context did contain both prices (FL-200P $59, UV-200 $35) and the 12-month UV-C interval, but not the chunk that says "every 4 months with pets, smoking or heavy cooking". Notebook: every 4 months, 3 × $59 = $177, plus $35 for the lamp, $212 per year, followed by a tip to clean the pre-filter every two weeks because of cat hair.

Q5 – Q1 units. RAG correct: it listed the monthly sums 1,240 / 1,180 / 1,370 and added them to 3,790. Notebook built a table with correct monthly totals and correct per-model totals (1,245 / 1,890 / 655), and then printed 3,490 both in its first sentence and in the table's total row. Since 1,245 + 1,890 + 655 = 3,790, this is a pure arithmetic slip on correctly retrieved numbers.

Q6 – Wi-Fi spike. Both correct: March, 47 tickets, firmware 2.3 causing E11 reconnect issues, fixed by 2.3.1 on 28 March. Notebook showed a collapsible "Thoughts" section on this answer (and on Q1) and took the longest of all ten, 13.8 s.

Q7 – Pro revenue. RAG refused. The top 5 were the two sales-report chunks, the two price-list chunks and the first user-manual chunk; the $349 list price lives in the second user-manual chunk (the lower half of the spec table). This is a classic trap: a "price list" looks like the right place for a price and scores high, but it only contains spare parts. Notebook listed all ten monthly Pro figures and calculated 3,285 × $349 = $1,146,465.

Q8 – Pro-only codes. Both correct. RAG answered in one sentence (E08 and E13). Notebook explained both, added the parts and prices (SN-VOC $29, UV-200 $35) and pointed out that E11 and E12 exclude only the Lite.

Q9 – Bathroom. RAG refused even though the answer appears twice in the corpus: in the safety notes and in the FAQ. Neither chunk reached the top 5; the best match (0.700) was the remainder of the FAQ. Notebook: no, not where humidity is above 80% or where it can be splashed, plus a grounded extra — moisture on the touch panel is a listed cause of E15.

Q10 – AQ-300. Both declined correctly. Notebook added that the sources only cover the AQ-200 series, listed the three real list prices, and offered to search the web — helpful, but a reminder that the product can reach beyond your sources if you accept the offer.

Two opposite failure modes

This is, to me, the most useful result of the whole test: the two systems fail in opposite directions. A strict minimal RAG fails loudly; a strong assistant fails quietly. The interactive site avoids the quiet failure for numbers because the arithmetic runs in JavaScript, but it carries its own risk: whatever was baked in at generation time is shown with the same confidence until someone reviews it.

9. Limitations of this test

To keep the conclusions honest, these are the limits I am aware of:

  • Small sample. Ten questions over four short PDFs (17 chunks). Real manuals run to hundreds of pages, where retrieval matters far more and both systems may behave differently.
  • One run each. Every question was asked once per system, and each site was generated once. Generative output varies between runs, and I did not measure that variance; the generation times (about 2.5 and 3.5 minutes) are single measurements too.
  • Scored by the author. I scored the answers myself against ground truth computed from the source data. The criteria were simple (correct facts, correct refusal), but there was no second reviewer.
  • Unequal models. The RAG used the cheapest model with a deliberately strict prompt; Notebook uses whatever model Google runs behind it, which I can neither see nor choose. This compares a minimal self-built RAG with a managed product, not model against model.
  • Minimal RAG by design. No table-aware parsing, hybrid search or reranking. A production RAG would close part of the gap; the walkthrough in section 7 shows which fix would address which miss.
  • Sites checked by sampling. I verified the items listed in sections 4 and 5 and recomputed the dashboard's ledger and KPIs, but I did not check every sentence of the 15 FAQ entries or every one of the 15 error-code cards.
  • Latency is not like-for-like. Notebook chat times were measured in the browser, RAG times inside a script. Site generation runs in the background, so its minutes do not block the user the way chat latency does.
  • Clean, fictional data. Invented documents are tidy and consistent. Real manuals contain scanned pages, images and contradictions that would test both approaches harder.

10. My take

1. This is not "better RAG"; it is a different kind of output. RAG answers questions; an interactive site answers a need. From the same four PDFs, Notebook worked out on its own that an error-code table should become a search box, a maintenance schedule should become a checklist, and a price list plus replacement intervals should become a cost calculator. I did not design any UI — something RAG never does.

2. "Freezing the data into code" is an underrated strength. The Notebook chat added 3,790 up to 3,490, while its own dashboard got it right. When I need a number for a client, I will trust an interactive page that I have reviewed once over a chat answer that is regenerated every time.

3. But a site is a snapshot, not a system. There is no API, no automatic update, and regenerating can produce a different layout. For living documents (a spec that changes every week), RAG or a real system is still needed.

4. You still have to review it. The site "enriched" the content (FAQ from 9 to 15 entries, a two-pack suggestion). Most of it is grounded in the sources, but if it becomes official documentation it needs a review pass, just like AI-written code.

5. Minimal RAG is easy; good RAG is not. My 200-line pipeline was beaten by the tables inside the PDFs. Notebook handled tabular PDFs far better out of the box — and that is exactly the hard part users never have to see.

6. Honest about sources, opinionated about meaning. The dashboard's footer named the three documents it used and the weighting it applied — more provenance than many hand-made dashboards I have seen. Yet the same page added causal language and month labels nobody asked for. For a first draft I consider that a strength; for a client deliverable it is a risk I have to manage.

7. The two approaches are complementary, not rivals. If I designed a product-support experience from scratch, I would generate the structured tools (lookup, calculator, dashboard) from stable data, and keep a retrieval-based chat next to them for the long tail of free-form questions, with calculations moved into code on both sides.

8. What would make me use it more. For team use I miss three things: regenerating a site from updated sources while keeping its layout, per-element links back to the source instead of a single "View 4 sources" button, and a supported way to move a reviewed site into our own repository and hosting. Until then I treat generated sites as high-quality prototypes, not as products.

11. How I will use this in my daily work

At a software development company my work includes writing technical blog posts and proposals, building demos for clients, and reading manuals, specs and materials that clients send. Concretely:

My own security rule: only public or anonymized documents go into Notebook. Internal and client documents are processed only with pipelines I control (like the RAG in section 6) and according to company policy.

One concrete workflow: from a public manual to a clickable prototype

  1. Check the material. Only documents that are public (published manuals, brochures, public API documentation) or that are anonymised and allowed by company policy to be processed by a cloud service.
  2. Create a dedicated notebook per client or topic, upload only those sources, and confirm the source count before prompting.
  3. Prompt for tools, not pages. As in section 4, name each section, its inputs and the calculation it should perform; vague prompts get generic pages.
  4. Review with the checklist below, in the same mindset as a code review.
  5. Share inside the team first. With clients, show the prototype in the meeting rather than handing over a link to a notebook.
  6. If the idea is accepted, rebuild it properly in our own stack (with RAG where free-form questions are needed), using the generated site as a living UI specification.

My review checklist before sharing a generated site

  • Recompute every number on KPI cards and totals from the source, with a short script or a spreadsheet (this caught the weighted averages in my test).
  • Spot-check at least one entry per section against the source document, including model-specific or customer-specific rules.
  • Look for content that was added or rewritten (like the FAQ growing from 9 to 15 entries) and decide whether it is acceptable.
  • Look for formula choices and causal wording, and accept, annotate or regenerate.
  • Confirm that nothing on the page comes from outside the sources, and that nothing on it is more sensitive than the sources themselves.

A prompt template I will reuse

Based on what went right and wrong in this test, this is the template I will start from. It is a refinement I have not yet tested; the last three lines target the FAQ rewriting, the formula choice and the missing per-section sources I observed.

Make me a site: an interactive [tool type] for [audience], based only on these sources.
Include:
(1) a lookup where I can type or pick [key] and see [fields];
(2) a side-by-side comparison of [items];
(3) a calculator that computes [result] from [inputs] - show the formula used;
(4) a searchable FAQ that uses only questions that appear in the sources.
Do not add facts, labels or explanations that are not in the sources.
Use simple averages unless I say otherwise.
Show which source document each section comes from. Clean, mobile-friendly design.

12. FAQ

Is "Make me a site" just RAG with a user interface on top? Not in practice. RAG produces an answer each time a question is asked; site generation produces an application once, with the data embedded in it. When someone uses the site, no model runs at all (see section 8).

Are the numbers on a generated site trustworthy? In my test every number I recomputed on both sites was correct, because the page calculates them in JavaScript. What still needs a human decision is which formula is used (weighted vs simple averages) and any added wording.

What happens when the documents change? The site does not update itself. You prompt again, and each prompt in my test produced a new item in Studio. For documents that change weekly, a RAG pipeline (rebuild in about 10 seconds here) is the better fit.

Who can see a generated site? It lives in the notebook's Studio under your Google account and opens in a sandboxed *.usercontent.goog frame; you choose whether to share it. That is also why I only use non-sensitive sources.

How much did this experiment cost? The RAG side cost $0.0062 for ten questions, plus embedding 4,430 tokens. The Notebook side was included in my PRO plan, which does not show per-run costs.

Is any of the data real? No. Product, company, prices, sales and support figures are all invented (section 3).

13. Conclusion

From four fictional PDF manuals, Gemini Notebook generated a working troubleshooting site and a sales and support dashboard in roughly 2.5 to 3.5 minutes each, without code. Every value I checked on both sites was correct, the cross-document joins (error code → part → price, units → list price) were done for me, and totals were reliable because the page computes them. On the same documents, a minimal RAG pipeline answered 6 of 10 questions — never inventing anything, but refusing four times when retrieval missed the right chunk — while the Notebook chat answered 9 of 10, its one error being a wrong sum that looked perfectly plausible.

The difference from traditional RAG is not a matter of accuracy alone: RAG is a question-answering service that stays current and integrates with other systems, while a Notebook site is a reviewed snapshot turned into a tool. I will use the second for prototypes, lookup pages and companion dashboards built from public material, and keep the first for living, sensitive or integrated knowledge.
